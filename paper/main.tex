\begin{abstract}
Variational quantum algorithms (VQAs) are limited by device noise, and error mitigation is the standard remedy on near-term hardware. Choosing a mitigation method for deployment requires comparing its true cost, yet the usual comparison hides it: it fixes the number of shots per circuit execution, while the methods differ sharply in how many executions they need per estimate. Zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE) needs three to five, Clifford data regression (CDR) more than thirty per target circuit, and a trained neural model exactly one; ZNE's accuracy further rests on a noise-scaling assumption that coherent device errors can violate. Inside a variational loop that evaluates thousands of parameter settings, it is this execution multiplicity, not the accuracy at unlimited shots, that decides which method is affordable. Our premise is that the comparison that matters is at \emph{equal total shot budget per evaluation point}. We present budget-conditioned neural error mitigation (NEM): one network corrects a single shot-limited measurement from circuit and device features, conditioned on the shot-noise scale so that one model serves every budget. We evaluate every method---including each ZNE fold point and each CDR training circuit---under a protocol that spends the same total shots per evaluation point. In simulation, where the model is trained and tested on matched noise distributions, NEM is the most accurate method at total budgets up to $2^{14}$ shots under coherent miscalibration (reducing error by 72--81\%) and the only method that beats unmitigated execution at those budgets under stochastic device noise (11--20\%); at $2^{16}$ shots CDR overtakes it (84\% versus 81\%). We further characterize when extrapolation fails: under control-offset miscalibration, folding ZNE returns a worse answer than no mitigation on 38--63\% of instances, and projecting its estimates onto the physical range removes divergent outliers but leaves that rate unchanged. On a real IBM Heron processor, folding ZNE is worse than no mitigation on up to 88\% of a small set of four-qubit instances. A model trained only on a digital twin of that processor transfers unreliably, while fine-tuning it on 120 device-measured circuits with classically computed labels yields a 39\% error reduction on 16 held-out instances from the same calibration epoch. Under structured noise, improvements hold from 4 to 20 qubits with a model retrained at each size. Which mitigation method is best, we argue, depends on the budget at which it is deployed.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} quantum error mitigation, variational quantum algorithms, neural networks, NISQ, zero-noise extrapolation, Clifford data regression
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Variational quantum algorithms such as the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) and the quantum approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA) are leading candidates for near-term quantum advantage, but their expectation-value estimates are biased by device noise \citep{preskill2018quantum,cerezo2021variational,bharti2022noisy,tilly2022variational}. Error mitigation aims to remove this bias without the qubit overhead of error correction \citep{temme2017error,cai2023quantum}. The most widely used technique, zero-noise extrapolation, amplifies noise by gate folding and extrapolates the measurements back to the zero-noise limit \citep{temme2017error,li2017efficient,giurgicatiron2020digital,kim2023evidence}. Learning-based methods such as Clifford data regression instead fit a noisy-to-ideal map on classically simulable circuits run on the same device \citep{czarnik2021error,strikis2021learning}.

These methods are usually compared at a fixed, generous number of shots \emph{per circuit execution}. That comparison hides a first-order practical distinction: the methods need very different numbers of executions per estimate. ZNE needs three to five per mitigated value. CDR runs one execution per training circuit---more than thirty---for every new target circuit. A neural model trained offline needs exactly one. Inside a VQA loop that evaluates thousands of parameter settings under a per-iteration shot budget, these multiplicities decide which method is usable, not the accuracy at unlimited shots. \citet{bultrini2023unifying} made the budget axis explicit for classical methods and found the ranking is budget-dependent; we extend that analysis to learned mitigation and make the budget an input of the model itself.

This paper makes four contributions.

\emph{(i) Budget-conditioned neural mitigation.} We train a neural error mitigation model that maps a single $S$-shot measured estimate, circuit-structure features, and device-characterization features to the ideal expectation value, and we condition it on the shot-noise scale $1/\sqrt{S}$ so that one network serves every budget (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\emph{(ii) An equal-budget evaluation protocol and Pareto analysis.} Every method receives the same total shot budget $B$ per evaluation point, split across the executions it requires, with every execution---including each ZNE fold point and each CDR training circuit---drawn as measured shots under the same frozen noise realization (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Under this protocol NEM is the most accurate method at $B\le 2^{14}$ under coherent miscalibration and the only method that beats unmitigated execution at $B\le 2^{14}$ under stochastic device noise; at $B=2^{16}$ CDR overtakes it in both regimes (Section~\ref{sec:pareto}).

\emph{(iii) A characterization of extrapolation failure.} Under physically motivated control-offset miscalibration \citep{krantz2019engineer}, unitary folding amplifies the coherent angle error while the observable responds non-monotonically. ZNE then underperforms unmitigated execution on 38--63\% of instances, and divergent fits produce estimates far outside the physical range. Projecting estimates onto that range removes the outliers but not the failure: the worse-than-unmitigated rate is unchanged and the mean improvement of exponential and adaptive ZNE stays negative in 25 of 28 (cell, variant) pairs across the structured regimes. We map this failure boundary against the miscalibration strength, quantify the tail risk (Section~\ref{sec:failure}), and observe the same failure on a real IBM Heron processor (Section~\ref{sec:hardware}).

\emph{(iv) Scaling and controls.} With a model retrained at each size, mean error reductions of 61--85\% under structured noise persist without degradation from 4 to 20 qubits, and a direct-prediction control---predicting $\Hideal$ from features alone, without the noisy measurement---never beats unmitigated execution at any size, confirming that mitigation, not memorization, drives the result (Sections~\ref{sec:scaling} and~\ref{sec:ablations}).

We are explicit about what NEM does not win. When every execution receives the same shot count---so that CDR spends $32\times$ NEM's total shots per evaluation---CDR is the most accurate method in every regime we study, and at equal total budget it overtakes NEM at $B=2^{16}$. The case for learned mitigation is not accuracy supremacy but its position on the low-budget end of the budget--accuracy frontier and its lighter empirical error tail; both are established in a matched-distribution setting that structurally favours a trained model (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Classical mitigation.}
ZNE \citep{temme2017error,li2017efficient} assumes the bias varies smoothly with a controllable noise-scale factor; digital implementations amplify noise by unitary folding \citep{giurgicatiron2020digital}, and the technique has produced headline demonstrations on hardware at the 100-qubit scale \citep{kim2023evidence}. Its reliability, however, is contingent on the extrapolation ansatz matching the actual noise response, a sensitivity we quantify in Section~\ref{sec:failure}. Probabilistic error cancellation \citep{temme2017error,van2023probabilistic} yields unbiased estimates at exponential sampling cost; virtual distillation \citep{huggins2021virtual,koczor2021exponential} suppresses errors using multiple state copies at additional quantum cost. CDR \citep{czarnik2021error} fits a linear noisy-to-ideal regression on near-Clifford circuits executed on the device, paying tens of executions per target circuit but inheriting robustness to noise structure because it calibrates on the device as it actually is; \citet{strikis2021learning} extend the idea with learning-based protocols, and \citet{czarnik2025improving} reduce its overhead with fewer training circuits and non-uniform shot allocation. \citet{bultrini2023unifying} benchmark these methods under a unified framework as a function of total shot budget and find that cheap methods win at small budgets while data-hungry methods win at large ones---the organizing observation our protocol builds on.

\paragraph{Learned mitigation.}
Neural networks have been used to reconstruct states and observables from noisy quantum data \citep{torlai2018neural} and to mitigate errors in quantum simulation \citep{bennewitz2022neural}. \citet{liao2023machine} benchmark random forests, multilayer perceptrons, and graph networks for expectation-value mitigation, including at the 100-qubit scale on hardware, and find learned models competitive with ZNE at lower quantum cost---an observation our protocol makes systematic. Closest to this work, \citet{cantori2024synergy} train convolutional networks to predict ideal expectation values of parameterized circuits from noisy values and circuit structure, including transfer to circuit sizes beyond the training set, and \citet{liao2025noiseagnostic} train noise-agnostic models with data-augmented training, outperforming ZNE and CDR on hardware at 4--6 qubits. \citet{adeniyi2025adaptive} adapt a neural mitigator to per-circuit error characteristics and validate it on IBM hardware. None of these studies fixes the total shot budget per evaluation point: comparisons grant every method full per-execution shot counts, and the per-evaluation execution multiplicity is not part of the analysis. Our contribution is orthogonal and composable with theirs: an equal-budget protocol, budget conditioning of the model itself, and an explicit failure-mode analysis of the classical alternative.

\section{Budget-conditioned neural mitigation}
\label{sec:method}

A VQA prepares $\ket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}=U(\boldsymbol{\theta})\ket{0}^{\otimes n}$ and estimates the objective $C(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\bra{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}H\ket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}$. On hardware one observes an $S$-shot estimate of the noisy expectation,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\expect{H}}_{\mathrm{noisy}} \;=\; \Hideal + \delta(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{E}) + \varepsilon_S,
\end{equation}
where $\delta$ is the bias induced by the noise process $\mathcal{E}$ and $\varepsilon_S$ is measurement noise with scale proportional to $1/\sqrt{S}$. We learn a correction function
\begin{equation}
f_\phi\!\left(\widehat{\expect{H}}_{\mathrm{noisy}},\, \mathbf{x}_c,\, \mathbf{x}_n,\, 1/\sqrt{S}\right) \;\approx\; \Hideal ,
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}_c$ collects circuit features (qubit count, layer count, parameter count, and the variational angles themselves; dimension $3+4n$ for the ansatz family used here) and $\mathbf{x}_n$ collects device-characterization features (gate error rates, readout asymmetries, and---when characterized---the calibration-drift scale; Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

A single $S$-shot noisy VQA execution is combined with circuit-structure and device-characterization embeddings---including the shot-noise scale $1/\sqrt{S}$---to predict the ideal expectation value with one classical forward pass. \paragraph{Architecture.}
Figure~\ref{fig:overview} shows the model. Two multilayer-perceptron encoders embed the feature groups: the circuit encoder maps $\mathbf{x}_c$ through hidden widths $[128,256]$ to a 128-dimensional embedding, and the noise encoder maps $\mathbf{x}_n$ through width 64 to a 64-dimensional embedding. The embeddings are concatenated with the measured value and passed through an output network of widths $[256,512,256]$ (LayerNorm, GELU, dropout $0.15$) that predicts a residual correction added to the input estimate. The standard configuration has 428K parameters; Section~\ref{sec:ablations} shows accuracy is insensitive to capacity over a $5\times$ range, so the capacity is generous rather than necessary.

\paragraph{Training.}
The model minimizes the Huber loss with AdamW (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, cosine annealing with warm restarts) for 75 epochs---about five minutes on a CPU workstation at the sizes studied. Training pairs $(\widehat{\expect{H}}_{\mathrm{noisy}}, \mathbf{x}_c, \mathbf{x}_n, \Hideal)$ are generated in simulation: parameters and noise realizations are sampled per example, the noisy input is an actual $S$-shot measured estimate, and the ideal label is computed by exact noiseless simulation. Ideal labels therefore restrict \emph{training} to classically simulable sizes, a limitation we return to in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\paragraph{Budget conditioning.}
We condition training on the shot-noise scale so that one model serves every deployment budget. Each training example draws its shot count $S$ log-uniformly from $2^{8}$ to $2^{16}$, samples the noisy input at that $S$, and appends $1/\sqrt{S}$ to $\mathbf{x}_n$. The input is intended to let the network learn how much of the input fluctuation is measurement noise versus learnable bias, so that no retraining is needed across budgets. We have not ablated it (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{Costs.}
The offline cost has three parts that we account for separately. (a) \emph{Classical simulation}: 4{,}400 simulated training circuits per device and noise regime, with exact labels; this costs no QPU time but grows exponentially with $n$ (about two minutes at $n{=}8$ and 8--13 wall-clock hours at $n{=}20$ on our workstation), plus a few minutes of training per seed. (b) \emph{Device characterization}: gate errors, $T_1/T_2$ and readout errors are read from the calibration data that the hardware provider already publishes for each calibration cycle, so they add no executions. The calibration-drift scale that the model receives in the miscalibration regime is not published; on hardware it would require its own characterization experiments, which we do not count. (c) \emph{Optional on-device data}: on real hardware we fine-tune on 120 device-measured circuits per calibration epoch (Section~\ref{sec:hardware}). Inference adds one classical forward pass (under one millisecond) to a single quantum execution. Table~\ref{tab:cost} summarizes per-evaluation costs against the baselines.

\paragraph{Break-even.}
Let $M$ be the number of distinct evaluation points---parameter settings, or target circuits---that a deployed model serves before it must be refreshed, and count QPU executions at equal per-execution shots. The baselines carry no offline cost but pay their multiplicity at every point: $kM$ for ZNE ($k=3$ or $4$) and $32M$ for CDR, which re-fits its near-Clifford regression for each target circuit. NEM's QPU cost depends on where its training data come from (Table~\ref{tab:breakeven}). If training uses simulated data and published calibration features alone, NEM spends one execution per point and no offline QPU time, but on our hardware run this configuration transferred unreliably (Section~\ref{sec:hardware}). If, as on our hardware run, it is fine-tuned on $F=120$ device-measured circuits per calibration epoch, it is cheaper from $M^\star=\lfloor F/(k-1)\rfloor+1$ points on within that epoch: 4 points relative to CDR and 41--61 relative to ZNE. Only in the worst case, where all 4{,}400 training circuits would have to be measured on the device, does the break-even rise to 142 points relative to CDR and 1{,}467--2{,}201 relative to ZNE. Three caveats apply. The classical cost of simulation and labelling, item (a) above, is not counted here and is what limits NEM's size. The CDR multiplicity of 32 reflects our configuration (31 training circuits with uniform shot allocation); more economical CDR variants \citep{czarnik2025improving} reduce it and shrink every break-even proportionally. And because the fitted correction is calibration-specific (Appendix~\ref{app:hardware}), the on-device cost recurs at each recalibration, so amortization holds only within an epoch. This count is at equal per-execution shots; the equal-budget protocol of Section~\ref{sec:setup} instead fixes the total shots per evaluation. The two are complementary: one asks how many evaluations repay the offline cost, the other which method is most accurate at a fixed per-evaluation budget.

\section{Experimental protocol}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Circuits and observables.}
We use hardware-efficient VQE ansatzes (alternating $R_Y,R_Z$ rotation layers and CNOT ladders, two layers) at $n=4$--$16$ qubits with the mean-magnetization observable $H=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i Z_i$. Simulations use Qiskit Aer \citep{qiskit2024}: exact density-matrix count sampling up to 10 qubits and statevector trajectory sampling above, so every reported measurement carries genuine sampling statistics. Classical baselines use mitiq~1.0 \citep{larose2022mitiq}.

\paragraph{Noise regimes.}
Three regimes span the noise-structure spectrum:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Stochastic device noise}: per-instance device profiles (depolarizing gate errors, thermal relaxation, asymmetric readout) sampled over realistic ranges---the regime where smooth noise-scaling assumptions are most plausible.
\item \emph{Stochastic-correlated noise}: adds per-instance gate-error fluctuations and correlated $ZZ$ crosstalk.
\item \emph{Coherent miscalibration}: models control-offset calibration error \citep{krantz2019engineer}. Working in the native gate basis $\{R_Z,\sqrt{X},X,\mathrm{CNOT}\}$, every physical pulse executes its nominal angle plus an offset drawn once per device snapshot from $\mathcal{N}(\delta,(\delta/2)^2)$ with $\delta\in[0.02,0.06]$\,rad, on top of two-qubit depolarizing noise and asymmetric readout---so the coherent offset overlays a stochastic background, which some figures label ``stochastic\,$+$\,coherent.'' Virtual $R_Z$ rotations, implemented as exact software frame updates, are exempt. Because the miscalibration lives in the executed unitary, folded ZNE circuits and CDR training circuits face \emph{the same} quantum process. Under folding, offsets in the inverted segment do not cancel---$R_Y(\theta{+}\delta)\,R_Y(-\theta{+}\delta)=R_Y(2\delta)$---so the coherent error grows linearly with the scale factor while the observable responds non-monotonically: the mechanism by which coherent errors defeat extrapolation.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Fairness protocol.}
Each test instance freezes one noise realization (a device snapshot). The raw measurement, the NEM input, every ZNE fold point, and every CDR training circuit are $S$-shot measured estimates under that same realization; ideal values are exact noiseless simulations. Baseline failures abort the run; there are no silent fallbacks. ZNE uses mitiq's Richardson, exponential ($y(\lambda)=a\,e^{-b\lambda}$ with the depolarizing asymptote), and adaptive-exponential factories with global folding at scale factors $\{1,2,3\}$; CDR uses 31 near-Clifford training circuits with half the non-Clifford gates retained. We report 150 test instances per configuration (100 for budget sweeps, 80 for the $\delta$-sweep of Figure~\ref{fig:boundary}) and paired $t$-tests against unmitigated execution, and because extrapolation has heavy-tailed failures we report median absolute errors alongside means throughout. Since the observable lies in $[-1,1]$, we also report every method after projecting its estimates onto that interval, a free post-processing step that can only reduce absolute error (Section~\ref{sec:failure}).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The equal-budget Pareto frontier}
\label{sec:pareto}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/budget_pareto.pdf}
\caption{Equal-budget comparison at $n=8$ (100 instances; median $|$error$|$, interquartile range shaded). Every method spends the same total budget $B$ per evaluation point, split across its required executions (Table~\ref{tab:cost}). NEM is the most accurate method at $B\le2^{14}$ under miscalibration and the only one below the unmitigated baseline at $B\le2^{14}$ under stochastic noise; CDR crosses below NEM at the largest budget. ZNE medians track the unmitigated line: for the typical instance extrapolation removes nothing, while its mean error diverges (Section~\ref{sec:failure}).}
\label{fig:pareto}
\end{figure}

Under equal-budget comparison, NEM is the most accurate method at low-to-moderate budgets and is overtaken at the largest budget we test. Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} shows this; Table~\ref{tab:budget_full} in the appendix lists every value. Under coherent-miscalibration noise, NEM reduces the mean error by 72\%, 77\%, 81\%, and 81\% at $B=2^{10},2^{12},2^{14},2^{16}$ respectively---nearly flat in $B$, because the learned correction targets the systematic bias, which does not shrink with more shots. CDR, whose per-execution shot count is $B/32$, climbs from $+23\%$ at $B=2^{10}$ to $+84\%$ at $B=2^{16}$, overtaking NEM ($+81\%$) at the largest budget. Both ZNE variants are \emph{negative} at essentially every budget in this regime ($-36\%$ to $-471\%$ in the mean for exponential ZNE, and below $-10^4\%$ for adaptive ZNE at $B=2^{10}$); the lone exception---adaptive ZNE at $B=2^{14}$ ($+7\%$ mean)---is a heavy-tail artifact whose median still sits at the unmitigated level. Projecting ZNE estimates onto $[-1,1]$ shrinks these losses to $-9\%$ to $-80\%$ but leaves every sign unchanged. Under stochastic device noise the picture compresses (the residual error is dominated by shot noise), but the ordering at low budget is the same: NEM is the only positive method at $B\le2^{14}$ ($+11$ to $+20\%$), and CDR turns positive only at $B=2^{16}$ ($+47\%$).

A VQE optimization loop typically evaluates $10^3$--$10^4$ parameter settings at a per-iteration budget of $10^3$--$10^4$ shots, which is the budget range where single-execution learned mitigation performs best in our simulations. Two qualifications apply. First, these are accuracies at random parameter points; we do not run a full VQE or QAOA optimization, so better point accuracy is not shown to yield better convergence or final solutions. Second, the crossover with CDR depends on CDR's configuration: a CDR variant with fewer training circuits or tuned shot allocation \citep{czarnik2025improving} spends more shots per execution and could move the crossover to lower budgets.

\subsection{When extrapolation fails, and how badly}
\label{sec:failure}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{figures/phase_boundary.pdf}
\caption{Median $|$error$|$ versus miscalibration strength $\delta$ at $n=8$ (80 instances per point, 8{,}192 shots per execution). ZNE medians coincide with the unmitigated baseline at every $\delta$, including $\delta=0$, where the residual bias is dominated by readout error that folding does not amplify. NEM matches CDR at small $\delta$ at $1/32$ the quantum cost, and its improvement declines smoothly as the unlearnable coherent share grows. A single NEM is trained once on $\delta\sim\mathcal{U}(0,0.09)$---wider than the $[0.02,0.06]$ range used in the other experiments---and evaluated across the sweep, so every point is in-distribution and no model is retrained per~$\delta$.}
\label{fig:boundary}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:boundary} sweeps the coherent bias $\delta$ from 0 to 0.08\,rad over a fixed stochastic background, and the methods separate cleanly. ZNE's \emph{median} improvement never exceeds $+2\%$ anywhere on the sweep---including at $\delta=0$, because the remaining bias there is dominated by readout error, which gate folding does not amplify; extrapolation removes the gate-error component and faithfully retains the rest.

Its \emph{mean} error, however, explodes as $\delta$ grows (for exponential extrapolation, $-646\%$ at $\delta=0.06$), driven by heavy tails. Table~\ref{tab:safety} shows the anatomy at one representative cell, and Table~\ref{tab:safety_full} in the appendix shows every structured-regime cell: ZNE returns a result worse than the unmitigated estimate on 38--63\% of instances, with single-instance errors reaching three orders of magnitude above the unmitigated level when the extrapolation fit diverges. Unconstrained extrapolation can leave the physical range: at $n{=}12$ we observe a single instance where the adaptive variant's error reaches $371$ in observable units, more than three orders of magnitude above the worst unmitigated error in that cell.

A simple constraint removes such outliers, so we test whether it also removes the failure. Because $\expect{H}\in[-1,1]$, projecting every estimate onto that interval costs no measurements and can only reduce absolute error. Table~\ref{tab:clip_full} in the appendix applies it to every structured-regime cell. The maximum ZNE error falls to at most $1.1$, and mean losses shrink sharply (adaptive ZNE at $n{=}12$ under miscalibration goes from $-6{,}250\%$ to $-22\%$). The worse-than-unmitigated rate is unchanged in every cell: projection affects at most 4\% of the estimates in any cell, and all 61 out-of-range estimates were worse than the raw value both before and after projection. The mean improvement of exponential and adaptive ZNE remains negative in 25 of the 28 (cell, variant) pairs. The failure therefore lies in the typical instance, not only in the tail. These results characterize ZNE with global unitary folding and the three standard mitiq factories at scale factors $\{1,2,3\}$; they do not establish that every extrapolation scheme must fail.

NEM, by contrast, tracks CDR at small $\delta$ ($+82\%$ versus $+83\%$ mean at $\delta=0$) at $1/32$ of the quantum cost. Its improvement then declines smoothly to $+44\%$ at $\delta=0.08$---still inside its $[0,0.09]$ training range, so this is genuine learning difficulty, not extrapolation---as the high-dimensional oscillatory component of the coherent bias exceeds what the network can learn from 4{,}000 samples. CDR, which re-fits per instance, stays at 69--86\%.

The neural model's error tail is lighter. Across all 2{,}000 structured-regime test instances (4--20 qubits), its worst-case error stays \emph{below} the worst unmitigated error in every cell, and it never leaves the physical range. This is an empirical property of the tested distributions, not a guarantee: the residual architecture places no bound on the size of the correction, and NEM is still worse than unmitigated execution on 7--19\% of instances (Table~\ref{tab:safety_full}).

The tail can be reduced further with a safeguard. We train three seeds and fall back to the raw value when they disagree beyond a threshold calibrated on unlabeled data. This drops the worse-than-raw rate from 7\% to 5\% at a cost of three points of mean improvement, and in the low-noise stochastic regime it pins the model's worst case exactly at the unmitigated level. We view the safeguard as cheap insurance rather than a core mechanism; because the seeds share training data, their agreement cannot rule out errors they make in common.

\subsection{Real-hardware validation}
\label{sec:hardware}

We test the failure analysis on a real device. We run the hardware-efficient VQE on \texttt{ibm\_marrakesh}, a 156-qubit Heron~r2 superconducting processor, using four qubits at two circuit depths (2 and 5 ansatz layers). We compare unmitigated execution against folding ZNE under the equal-budget protocol, with ten and eight test instances respectively, 8{,}192 shots, and all jobs grouped in a single batch. No noise is injected---the device \emph{is} the noise source.

Figure~\ref{fig:hardware} shows the result: folding ZNE fails on the real device as it does in simulation. Its mean error is $3.0\times$ the unmitigated error at two layers and $4.1\times$ at five layers, and it returns a worse answer than no mitigation on 80\% and 88\% of instances respectively; the deeper circuit, with more gates to fold, fails harder. The run does not identify the cause. Folding triples circuit duration, so decoherence and stochastic gate error accumulate alongside any coherent control offset, and either could break the extrapolation. The hardware result is therefore consistent with the coherent mechanism isolated in simulation (Section~\ref{sec:failure}) but does not uniquely attribute the failure to it. With ten and eight instances on four qubits of one device, it shows that the failure occurs on real hardware, not how often.

The neural mitigator's headline improvements are established in simulation, where training and test share the noise process. On hardware, a model trained purely on a digital twin transfers unreliably: the checkpoint used in this run is worse than unmitigated on 60\% and 88\% of the same instances (Figure~\ref{fig:hardware}), whereas a later twin checkpoint scores $+16\%$ on a new draw of instances and $+40\%$ when re-applied to the five-layer instances (Appendix~\ref{app:hardware}). We attribute this gap primarily to the twin's mismatch rather than to learned mitigation itself---consistent with the affine fit below recovering a shrinkage factor below one (the twin overcorrects)---though with a single device we cannot fully separate the two. Fine-tuning the twin-trained model on a small on-device set---120 four-qubit circuits measured in a single batched job, labelled by exact classical simulation---raises its error reduction on 16 held-out instances on \texttt{ibm\_marrakesh} from $+16\%$ (twin-only) to $+39\%$, all from the same calibration epoch. The gain is stable across hyperparameters and training seeds, though these vary the training rather than the independent test sample, and a deterministic affine recalibration of the twin's correction reaches $+45\%$ on this epoch (Appendix~\ref{app:hardware}). Both follow the route of using device data that prior learned mitigators take \citep{liao2025noiseagnostic,adeniyi2025adaptive}. With a small amount of same-epoch device data the learned correction is beneficial in aggregate, though still worse than unmitigated on about a quarter of individual instances and calibration-specific (Appendix~\ref{app:hardware}). We regard this as a feasibility result on one device and one epoch, not as a hardware accuracy claim.

\subsection{Scaling}
\label{sec:scaling}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/scaling.pdf}

NEM's improvement does not degrade as circuits grow from 4 to 20 qubits. Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} shows this; Table~\ref{tab:scaling_full} in the appendix lists every cell. Mean error reduction under coherent miscalibration is 72\%, 77\%, 80\%, 85\%, 76\%, 80\%, 77\% at $n=4,6,8,10,12,16,20$, and the stochastic-correlated regime is similarly flat (61--68\%); in both structured regimes every NEM improvement is significant ($p<10^{-4}$, paired $t$-test), with no degradation up to the largest size simulated. At $n{=}16$ under miscalibration, NEM's $+80\%$ matches CDR's $+81\%$, and at $n{=}20$ its $+77\%$ matches CDR's $+79\%$, each at $1/32$ of the quantum cost. The per-size models use identical architecture and training budgets; only the input dimension grows, linearly in $n$. Each size therefore has its own model trained on exactly labelled data at that size: the results show that the method can be retrained at larger sizes without loss, not that one model transfers across sizes, and the exact labels confine training to classically simulable sizes.

Under purely stochastic noise the headroom itself shrinks: the unmitigated error approaches the shot-noise floor of the per-execution budget ($S{=}8{,}192$ up to $n{=}10$; $1{,}024$ at $n{=}12,16$; $512$ at $n{=}20$, chosen for simulation cost). NEM's improvement then tracks that ceiling: $+10$ to $+22\%$ ($p<0.05$) through $n=12$, $+7\%$ ($p=0.12$, not significant) at $n=16$, and $+11\%$ ($p=0.026$) at $n=20$. More than half of the residual error there is irreducible measurement noise, so little bias is left for any single-execution method to remove.

\subsection{Controls and ablations}
\label{sec:ablations}

\paragraph{Direct prediction.}
A skeptical reader should ask whether the network is mitigating or merely memorizing the expectation-value landscape. A control model with identical architecture but no access to the noisy measurement answers this: it is worse than unmitigated execution in every cell---never beating it at any size or regime, by as much as $927\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:scaling_full}). The measurement is indispensable; the network's role is correction, not regression of $\Hideal$ from circuit parameters.

\paragraph{Features.}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} isolates what the network uses, at $n=8$ under miscalibration. Device-characterization features carry the conditioning signal: removing them triples the error ($+191\%$ MAE), while removing circuit features costs almost nothing ($+0.3\%$) in this regime. The interpretation is physical: the learnable bias components---stochastic shrinkage, readout offset, and the mean effect of the drift $\delta$---are functions of the device state, whereas the angle-dependent fine structure of the coherent bias is precisely the part the network cannot extract from 4{,}000 samples. Even with both feature groups removed, the measurement-conditioned model retains $+46\%$; the direct-prediction control shows the converse, that features without the measurement are worthless. The near-zero cost of removing circuit features also means that in this regime much of the benefit is a device-conditioned recalibration of the measured value. The direct-prediction control establishes that the measurement matters, not that a deep nonlinear correction is needed; we have not compared against a standalone affine or low-order regression with the same inputs, and on hardware an affine recalibration of the twin's correction matches or exceeds fine-tuning (Appendix~\ref{app:hardware}).

\paragraph{Capacity.}
The concern that a 400K-parameter network is overparameterized for a roughly 40-dimensional input is correct---and harmless: an 88K-parameter model matches the 428K standard exactly ($+81.4\%$ versus $+81.7\%$ mean error reduction on identical data), so capacity can be cut $5\times$ without loss.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What we claim.}
At equal per-evaluation quantum budget and under matched training and test noise, single-execution learned mitigation occupies the low-budget end of the accuracy--cost frontier and has a lighter empirical error tail than folding ZNE; and we characterize---rather than assert---a regime where folding ZNE is worse than no mitigation even after its estimates are projected onto the physical range.

\paragraph{Scope of the comparison with ZNE.}
The comparison is structurally favourable to NEM. In simulation the model is trained and tested on closely matched noise distributions and receives device-characterization features, whereas ZNE uses no labelled training data and no prior knowledge of the device. Our results therefore show that a trained model outperforms folding ZNE \emph{when such training data and features are available and representative}; they do not show general superiority over ZNE. The hardware experiment illustrates the point: without device data, the twin-trained model transfers unreliably. In the miscalibration regime the model also receives the drift scale, which on a real device would itself have to be characterized.

\paragraph{What we do not claim.}
CDR is more accurate wherever its $32\times$ per-evaluation cost is affordable; our results quantify, not contest, that trade. We claim no formal bound on NEM's error, only empirical tail behaviour on the tested distributions. Nor do we claim architectural novelty: the network is a plain residual MLP, and the contribution lies in the budget conditioning, the evaluation protocol, and the failure analysis.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Several limitations bound these results and mark the directions we see as most promising. Our evaluation is primarily in simulation; we validate the ZNE failure mode on real hardware (Section~\ref{sec:hardware}), and the neural mitigator's headline improvements are established in simulation. A model trained purely on a digital twin transfers unreliably to hardware; a small on-device fine-tuning set narrows the gap on 16 held-out instances from one device and one calibration epoch (Appendix~\ref{app:hardware}). The fitted correction does not transfer reliably across epochs, so it must be refreshed at each recalibration, which limits amortization. Broader hardware validation across devices, epochs and larger sample sizes is needed. The noise models, while physically motivated (calibration offsets, crosstalk, readout asymmetry), are otherwise not device-fitted. Training labels require exact classical simulation, restricting training to classically simulable sizes; transfer from small training circuits to larger deployment circuits \citep{cantori2024synergy,strikis2021learning} and label-free data augmentation \citep{liao2025noiseagnostic} are the natural extensions for further reducing the device data the on-device fine-tuning above requires, and both compose with our budget conditioning. We study a single diagonal observable (mean magnetization); non-diagonal Hamiltonians require basis-rotated measurement and may change feature design. We report accuracy at random parameter points and do not run complete VQE or QAOA optimizations. Our baselines are standard configurations: we do not evaluate standalone affine, low-order regression or readout-correction baselines with the same inputs, nor the more economical CDR variants of \citet{czarnik2025improving}, and the model's sensitivity to inaccurate noise features is untested. Nor have we ablated the budget input against the same network trained on mixed budgets without it, or against separate per-budget models, so the specific benefit of budget conditioning over these alternatives remains to be established. Each model is trained per system size, device family and noise regime; drift beyond the characterized feature ranges requires retraining, though the design of shot-budget conditioning suggests how broader conditioning could be added. Finally, our ansatz family is fixed; generalization across circuit families is untested here and is where graph- or sequence-structured encoders would plausibly earn their complexity.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Mitigation methods should be compared the way they are deployed: under a fixed quantum budget per evaluation point. Under that accounting, and when representative training data are available, a budget-conditioned neural mitigator---one measured estimate in, one corrected estimate out---is in simulation the most accurate method at the low budgets that VQA optimization loops occupy, has an empirically lighter error tail than folding extrapolation, and concedes the high-budget regime to Clifford data regression. We believe this budget-aware framing, more than any single architecture, is the durable contribution: it turns ``which mitigation method is best?'' into the answerable question ``best at what price?''.

\section{Hardware sim-to-real study}
\label{app:hardware}

Section~\ref{sec:hardware} reports the ZNE failure on \texttt{ibm\_marrakesh} and notes that a purely twin-trained neural mitigator transfers unreliably. We give the full picture here---first the gap under the hardest, no-device-data setting, then the small amount of on-device data that narrows it---because both halves are informative about when and how learned mitigation can be deployed.

Our hardware protocol is sim-to-real by construction: we read the device calibration through the runtime \texttt{Target} (gate errors, $T_1/T_2$, readout), build an Aer noise model from it with \texttt{NoiseModel.from\_backend}, train the budget-conditioned model on that digital twin, and test on the physical processor. This is the hardest deployment setting---no device data enters training---and it exposes a clean distribution shift. Table~\ref{tab:hardware} reports the outcome. The twin's depolarizing channels overstate the device's true bias, so the model learns a correction that is too aggressive: on the near-shot-noise four-qubit circuits it overshoots the (already small) error, scoring $-3\%$ at two layers and $-94\%$ at five, where the deeper circuit's larger learned correction overshoots more.

Two considerations qualify the negative result. We attribute the gap primarily to the \emph{twin} rather than the method: in simulation, where training and test share the noise process (the deployment setting CDR and on-device methods also assume), the model is strongly positive (Sections~\ref{sec:pareto}--\ref{sec:scaling}). The hardware experiment swaps a matched noise model for a mismatched one, and the model degrades accordingly---the expected behaviour of a supervised corrector under covariate shift. The ensemble safeguard of Section~\ref{sec:failure} could limit such failures by gating large-disagreement corrections back to the raw value, but we have not tested it on hardware, and seeds trained on the same twin can share the same bias.

\paragraph{Narrowing the gap with on-device fine-tuning.}
A small amount of device data narrows the gap. We measure a fine-tuning set of 120 random four-qubit, five-layer circuits on \texttt{ibm\_marrakesh} in a single batched job, compute their exact ideal labels by classical statevector simulation, and fine-tune the twin-trained model on these real (noisy, ideal) pairs. We evaluate on 16 held-out instances measured in the same job, so training and test share one calibration epoch. Table~\ref{tab:hardware_ft} reports the outcome. On this draw the twin-only model already improves by $+16\%$ over unmitigated execution; fine-tuning lifts it to $+39\%$, and halves the worse-than-unmitigated rate from $50\%$ to $25\%$. The single best-seed run reaches $+56\%$; we report the more conservative mean over all settings. The gain is stable, not a lucky configuration: across $18$ learning-rate/epoch/encoder-freezing settings, each run over five seeds ($90$ runs), every configuration mean is positive (range $+27\%$ to $+41\%$) and the worst individual seed still reduces error by $+20\%$. A two-parameter affine recalibration of the twin's correction, $\langle H\rangle_{\mathrm{pred}}=\langle H\rangle_{\mathrm{noisy}}+a\,(\langle H\rangle_{\mathrm{twin}}-\langle H\rangle_{\mathrm{noisy}})+b$ fitted on the same device set, reaches $+45\%$ with $a=0.77<1$---a direct measurement that the twin overcorrects, exactly as the negative result diagnosed.

We are explicit about the scope of this correction: it is specific to a calibration epoch. The twin-only and fine-tuned models above are compared \emph{within} one epoch---training and held-out circuits measured in a single job---which is the comparison a deployment faces. Across epochs the fit does not transfer: applying this epoch's fine-tuned model to the earlier five-layer instances of Table~\ref{tab:hardware} (recomputed from their recorded measurements; the script is in the repository) yields a smaller improvement than the current twin, because the device has drifted and those near-shot-noise circuits leave little headroom. On those same instances the \emph{current} twin checkpoint scores $+39.5\%$, so Table~\ref{tab:hardware}'s $-94\%$ reflects an earlier twin and calibration rather than intrinsic instability of the method; we therefore report the within-epoch gain only. This epoch dependence is shared with every device-data method: CDR re-fits on freshly drawn device circuits for each target circuit, paying the cost per evaluation, whereas a fine-tuned model amortizes one device-data fit across an entire evaluation loop and is refreshed only when calibration drifts. A small, classically labelled on-device set is thus enough to turn the learned mitigator from a marginal corrector into a beneficial one on this epoch's held-out set---the route the negative result pointed to~\citep{liao2025noiseagnostic,adeniyi2025adaptive}.

\section{Reproducibility details}
\label{app:repro}

\paragraph{Data generation.} Each scaling cell uses 4{,}000 training and 400 validation pairs (2{,}500/250 at $n{=}20$), with circuit parameters drawn uniformly from $[0,2\pi)$ and noise parameters drawn per sample from the regime ranges of Section~\ref{sec:setup}. Noisy inputs are measured estimates at the per-size shot budget; ideal labels are exact statevector expectation values.

\paragraph{Training.} AdamW, learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, batch size 64, Huber loss, cosine annealing with warm restarts ($T_0{=}25$), 75 epochs, gradient clipping at norm 1. The best-validation checkpoint is selected. Three seeds are trained per cell; the best-validation seed is reported and the seed ensemble feeds the safeguard analysis.

\paragraph{Baselines.} mitiq 1.0 with global unitary folding at scale factors $\{1,2,3\}$ (adaptive: four steps, scale factor 2, depolarizing asymptote). CDR: 31 near-Clifford training circuits, fraction of non-Clifford gates retained $0.5$, target circuits transpiled to the $\{R_Z,\sqrt{X},X,\mathrm{CNOT}\}$ basis with an exact expectation-equivalence check. Every baseline execution passes through the same measured-shot estimator and, in the miscalibration regime, the same per-snapshot circuit transform as the raw measurement.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Improvements are reported against the unmitigated estimate on the same instances; significance uses two-sided paired $t$-tests on absolute errors. Medians and interquartile ranges are reported wherever extrapolation tails dominate means. Physical-range projection (Table~\ref{tab:clip_full}) is computed from the saved per-instance estimates by \texttt{experiments/scripts/clip\_analysis.py}.
